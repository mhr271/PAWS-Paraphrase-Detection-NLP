Same words, different meaning

PAWS builds non-paraphrases by scrambling word order and swapping entities, so two sentences can share almost every word and still disagree. These are the results of five experiments on that problem, from word counts up to a fine-tuned transformer.

Sentence 1

Panaon Island is a small island in the Philippines , in Southern Leyte province .

Sentence 2

Southern Leyte is a small island in the Philippines , in the province of Panaon Island .

The two place names trade positions between the sentences: Panaon Island and Southern Leyte.

Test accuracy, 1,000-pair test subset

Word-overlap features barely beat guessing the majority class. Fine-tuning does better, but not by much.

What the n-gram results show

Many n-grams can be found in both paraphrase and non paraphrase pairs, as shown by the frequency and TF-IDF results. Common words like "the," "in," and "of" still rank highly, but TF-IDF changes how important each one is. Bigram rankings stay mostly the same, but trigram rankings change more noticeably, with phrases like "river is a" and "river in romania" becoming more common. IDF is calculated for both classes, so just because an n-gram has a high TF-IDF weight doesn't mean it can tell the difference between paraphrases and non-paraphrases.

The overlap of one word phrases is almost the same for paraphrases and non-paraphrases (0.8858 vs. 0.8845). Paraphrases, on the other hand, have more bigram overlap (0.6094 vs. 0.5008) and trigram overlap (0.4656 vs. 0.3143). This means that longer n-grams are more useful because they show information about the order of the words. However, word level representations can't fully capture the meaning of a sentence. This is especially true in PAWS, where sentences can have similar words put together in different orders.

Reading the confusion matrix

The confusion matrix shows that the false negative is the most common type of mistake, with 315 of them and 120 of them being false positives. This means that the classifier sees a lot of real paraphrases as not paraphrases. This is also shown by the low recall value of 0.2708, which means that the model can only correctly spot about 27% of the real paraphrase pairs.

One reason could be that the three numerical features shared words, shared bigrams, and difference in sentence length don't show everything. These are the things that two sentences have in common as pairs. They don't fully show what the sentence means or how the words fit together grammatically. In the PAWS dataset, pairs of paraphrased and unparaphrased words can have very similar vocabularies, with only small changes in word order changing what they mean.

A score of 0.5650 on the test is also close to the percentage of cases in the test subset that did not use paraphrasing (56.8%). This shows that the classifier has trouble telling the difference between paraphrases and original text. Better representations that show sentence structure, word order, and meaning could cut down on these mistakes.For example, using contextual sentence embeddings from models such as Sentence-BERT could help the classifier understand relationships between words instead of relying only on shared words and sentence length.

Did fine-tuning change the alignment?

The attention analysis uses the same pair of correctly labelled sentences to compare the original pretrained DistilBERT model with the model that was fine tuned. The three words chosen from sentence1 are "Panaon," "Island," and "Philippines." The punctuation mark "." in sentence2 got the most attention from both models for all three words.

Some changes happen to the data when punctuation is taken out. The most attention was paid to "pan" (0.0350) for "Panaon" by the original model, and it remained "pan" (0.0140) after fine-tuning. The token that got the most attention for "Island" changed from "island" (0.0250) to "leyte" (0.0249). For "Philippines," it changed from "philippines" (0.0147) to "leyte" (0.0290). This shows that fine tuning changed the non punctuation alignments for two of the three selected words.

But these results don't always mean that fine tuning helped the model understand how sentences relate to each other, since attention weights alone can't fully explain how it behaves. The amount of training loss went down to 0.3949, and test accuracy reached 0.6220, which is higher than the 0.5680 baseline for the majority class. This means the model learned some useful patterns, but it still gets things wrong when it comes to classification. The performance could be improved by using more training data, adjusting the learning rate and batch size, and testing different hyperparameters using a validation set. This could help the model better identify paraphrase pairs and reduce classification errors.
